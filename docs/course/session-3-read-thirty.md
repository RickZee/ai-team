# Session 3 — Read thirty of them yourself

**40 minutes to read. One afternoon to do.** The session that decides whether any
of the others matter.

---

## The idea

You have a pile of traces. Now someone has to read some.

Not score them. Not run them through a model. **Read** them, one at a time, and
write down in your own words what went wrong. Thirty of them. By hand.

This is called **open coding**, it is borrowed from qualitative social science,
and the practitioners who teach this subject say they spend 60–80% of their
development time on it and the analysis around it.

The process, in three steps:

1. **Open coding.** Read a trace. Find the first place it went wrong. Describe it
   in your own words — "forgot the constraint from the brief", "tested the wrong
   file", "gave up and asked a human for no reason". Don't use a category list.
   You don't have one yet. That's the point.
2. **Axial coding.** After about thirty, your own notes start repeating. Cluster
   them. "forgot the constraint" and "dropped the pinned requirement" are one
   thing wearing two names. Give each cluster a name you'd defend in a meeting.
3. **The taxonomy.** Those clusters, written down with ids, are your list of
   failure modes. *Now* you know what to build checks for — and every category
   points back at the specific traces that produced it.

## Why a model cannot do this for you

This is the one hard rule in the whole course, so here is the mechanism rather
than the instruction.

If the tool shows you a suggestion — a category, a guess, a ranked list — you
will anchor on it. Not because you are weak, because that is what anchoring is.
You will confirm the model's framing more often than you'll invent your own, and
your notes stop being independent observations. They become a slightly edited
copy of what the model already thought.

And the model's framing came from its training data, not from your system.

Your notes are the **ground truth** — the thing every score downstream is
measured against. Ground truth generated by a model is not ground truth; it is
the model's opinion with a certificate. Every rate computed against it is
circular, and you can't detect that from the numbers, because the numbers look
fine.

So: during open coding, the tool shows you the trace and **nothing else**. No
suggestions, no categories, no autocomplete from a taxonomy. Reuse of your own
earlier notes is fine — those are yours. Anything from a model is not.

> assistants not to "helpfully" add AI assistance to it. Its author thinks it is
> the best paragraph in the codebase, and given what the rest of this course is
> about, he is probably right.

## Thirty, not three hundred

Thirty is a real number, not a round one. You are reading until **saturation** —
the point where new traces stop producing new categories. In practice the curve
flattens somewhere around twenty to thirty for most systems. Read past it and
you're spending afternoons to confirm what you already know.

## Diversity is not volume

The most expensive mistake in this session, and it is subtle enough to fool
someone who knows better.

The guidance says "roughly 100 diverse traces." The word carrying all the weight
is **diverse**. Here is a corpus of fifty:

```
sources ......... 1 value
outcomes ........ 1 value
scenarios ....... 1 value, and that value is "unknown"
time window ..... 1.97 seconds
```

That is not fifty samples. That is **one sample, recorded fifty times.** Every
rate computed from it has n=1 hiding inside an n that reads 50, and every
confidence interval is a decoration.

Before you read anything, group your corpus by every dimension you have —
model, configuration, scenario, outcome, day. If one value dominates a column,
your n is a fiction and no amount of reading fixes it. Go get more varied data
first.

That project had the varied data on disk the whole time. 210 records, three
systems, 69 days. The re-index that would have surfaced it cost nothing and had
been available since the day the code was written.

## What it cost — and this is the real lesson of the whole course

Thirteen thousand lines of eval infrastructure. Sampling strategies, an
annotation interface, a clusterer, split discipline, alignment statistics, bias
correction. All working. All tested.

Traces read by a human: **zero.**

The author's own summary:

> The people I learned this from say they spend 60–80% of development time on
> error analysis and evaluation. I have spent close to 100% of my eval time on
> *evaluation infrastructure* and 0% on *error analysis*. Those are different
> activities. The first is engineering, which I am good at and enjoy. The second
> is sitting down and reading traces for four hours, which is boring, and cannot
> be delegated to a model without destroying the thing it produces.
>
> I built the tool that makes the boring thing efficient, and then did not do the
> boring thing, and the tool was good enough that its existence felt like
> progress.

**Building the measurement is the most comfortable way to avoid doing the
measurement.** It feels like the same activity. It produces commits, tests,
diagrams, and a sense of movement. It is not the same activity, and the gap can
stay open for months because everything on the infrastructure side keeps going
green.

If you take one thing from this course, take this one.

## Your move

Block a two-hour slot. Really block it — this is the item that gets rescheduled
forever, by everyone, and that is the finding.

1. Group your traces by every dimension you have. If one value dominates, stop
   and go get more varied data.
2. Pick thirty, spread across whatever variety you have.
3. Read them. Write notes in your own words. Do not use a category list.
4. When notes repeat, cluster them and name the clusters.

Thirty traces read by hand will change what you build next more than any amount
of additional infrastructure.

---

## In one line

**A human reads thirty traces, unaided, and writes notes in their own words —
because that is the ground truth everything else is measured against, and it is
the one step that cannot be delegated or skipped without invalidating everything
downstream.**
